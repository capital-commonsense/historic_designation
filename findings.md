# Findings

Generated by `make all` from raw snapshot 2026-10-17; every figure is recomputed on rebuild. Rates cover only fiscal years with adequate coverage (coverage.md). Charts are in charts/out/.

## H1 HPRB designates most of the nominations it decides

**Supported, with a qualification.** In the 13 fiscal years where the scrape covers at least 80% of HPO's reported applications (FY2001–2020, gaps excluded), HPRB designated or amended 214 of 239 (90%) of nominations it decided; landmarks alone 190 of 212 (90%). Historic districts: 10 of 11 (91%) designated in those years; across all years the table records 49 district designations, 0 denials and 1 withdrawal. The qualification, and the strongest counterexample: nominations heard while a raze application was pending (H2) were designated only 6 of 15 (40%) (FY2007–25). Withdrawals before a hearing are undercounted, which flatters the rate.

## H2 Nominations are often filed while a permit application is pending

**Partly supported; a lower bound.** Of 271 individual-landmark nominations HPRB acted on in FY2007–25, HPRB agendas, actions, HPO notices or staff reports show a raze application pending at the hearing for 15 (6%) and an alteration or new-construction application for 13 more — 28 of 271 (10%) in all. Only two documents say which came first; both say the raze came first (St. Paul's Methodist Episcopal Church South: the raze application "precipitated the landmark nomination"; Barry Farm). DOB permit data add circumstantial cases: 9 of 25 nominations denied or withdrawn since 2009 were followed within 180 days by a raze permit at the address, 4 of them not otherwise documented. Nominations racing a raze fared differently: designated 40% versus 93% with no documented pending application. Limits: agendas list a raze only when HPO scheduled it "if designated"; the open permit data list issued permits only (2009 on), with no application dates.

## H3 Nominations are withdrawn in exchange for concessions

**Inconclusive: the public record cannot test it.** The nominations table holds 6 withdrawals and 2 nominations noticed but never heard. A check of every case number in HPRB agendas, actions and HPO monthly notices (2006–2026) found only three nominations HPRB never acted on; nominations withdrawn before HPO scheduled them leave no public trace. Of 7 agreements recorded in inputs/agreements.csv, 1 documents a withdrawal in exchange for concessions: St. Martin's (2006), where DCPL agreed to withdraw its application in return for preservation commitments and prior review of future permits, with no payment. Testing H3 needs HPO's case log and withdrawal letters (records_request/request.md).

## H4 Designations are rarely reversed

**Supported.** Against 897 designations and amendments in the table, it records 2 revocations: Kingman (President Monroe) Apartments (1997); Yellow Tavern (2022). No post-listing boundary reduction was found in the HPRB actions read; boundary history before the published current polygons is not rebuilt (coverage.md), so reductions in older districts cannot be ruled out.

## H5 Most nominations are filed by third parties, and owner objection does not affect the outcome

**First half supported; second half not.** For decisions in FY2010–22, when HPO's updates name nominators, 173 of 211 nominations have a known filer: third parties (preservation and neighborhood groups, ANCs, others) filed 98 of 173 (57%), owners filed or co-filed 51 of 173 (29%), government agencies 24 of 173 (14%). Filer types are inferred from names. By today's assessed value of what they designated (linked for 82% of these designations), government filings cover 42%, DCPL 25% and owners 10%; districts dominate: George Washington University/Old West End Historic District ($2.5B, filed by DC Historic Preservation Office); Kingman Park Historic District ($2.2B, filed by Kingman Park Civic Association); Bloomingdale Historic District ($2.1B, filed by DC Preservation League; Bloomingdale Historic Designation Coalition). Owner objection appears to matter: HPO's own annual tables for FY2007–22 count 12 owner-opposed nominations approved and 18 denied — an approval rate of 40%, against about 90% overall (H1). These are HPO's printed counts, not matched case by case; the tables' totals do not always sum.

## H6 The Mayor's Agent often grants permits over HPRB's objection, and decides slowly

**Override real: supported. Slow: mostly not.** Georgetown Law's collection holds 202 Mayor's Agent decisions (1978–2024; not a certified census). On the merits the Mayor's Agent granted 116 of 196 (59%): special merit 50 of 67 (75%), economic hardship 7 of 48 (15%). Where HPRB (or its predecessors) recommended against the owner, the permit was granted anyway in 53 of 122 (43%); for demolitions, 33 of 48 (69%). Where HPRB was favorable, 46 of 50 (92%). Timing: median 92 days from the last hearing session to the order (middle half 42–186; n=135; 8 over a year); 175 of 201 orders came in the application's fiscal year or the next. That covers only the Mayor's Agent stage, not delay before it. Appeals: owners lost all 8 Court of Appeals reviews of Mayor's Agent denials found; when preservation or neighborhood groups appealed a Mayor's Agent approval (15 cases), they won 1, got 3 remands and 1 mixed result, and lost 10. Opponents of an HPRB-approved alteration have no Mayor's Agent hearing as of right (requests refused in 1999, 2013 and 2014) and no Court of Appeals review (Dupont Circle Citizens Ass'n v. Barry, 1983).

## H7 HPRB rarely departs from HPO staff recommendations

**Departures are rare and run mostly against designation.** HPO staff reports posted for 122 nominations give a recommendation on 114 that HPRB decided (FY2015–2024); HPO recommended granting 107 and denying 7. HPRB departed 6 times (5%): it denied 4 that HPO supported (West Heating Plant, Charles Whitney Gilmore Residence, Harrison Street Flats, St. Paul’s Methodist Episcopal Church South) — 2 of them with a raze application pending (H2) — and granted 2 that HPO opposed (Barry Farm Dwellings, Kingman Park Historic District amendment (boundary expansion)). HPRB followed 5 of 7 HPO recommendations to deny. Staff reports are posted mostly from FY2014 on, so earlier years are untested; withdrawn and continued cases are not scored.

## H8 Designated property holds a disproportionate share of DC's property value

**Supported, more weakly once exempt property is removed.** Designated property is 28% of DC tax records but 41% of assessed value and 49% of land value. Excluding tax-exempt property (federal, District, institutional), the value share falls to 34% against 28% of records. By ward, value share exceeds record share most in Ward 5 (+12 points) and least in Ward 2 (-8 points); Ward 2 is 74% designated by records. Units are tax records (condo units count separately); boundaries and values are today's.

## H9 Most designated value was protected early, and additions have slowed

**Supported.** 78% of today's designated assessed value was protected by 1990, and 80% of value inside districts lies in districts first designated before 1990. Share of all DC assessed value added per decade since: 1990s +3.8%, 2000s +2.3%, 2010s +2.7%. Caveat: only current boundaries are published, so the 10 districts with recorded expansions count expansion lots from their original year, which overstates early protection.

## H10 Nominators have received payments in settlements

**Partly supported for 2003–2010; no documented case since.** inputs/agreements.csv records 2 payments to DCPL or a DCPL-run fund: Third Church of Christ, Scientist (2010, up to $770K to DCPL-managed fund, 20% administrative fee; terms per contemporaneous press summary of the filed agreement ($450K + $2/sq ft built, max $770K, to a DCPL-managed fund; DCPL may take 20% as an administrative fee); agreement text not yet obtained); Potomac Place (2003, $450K to DCPL (Monument Fund); unverified). Other cash mitigation went elsewhere: Washington Convention Center (FY1999, $1,000K to DC SHPO-administered fund). The Monument payment does not appear in DCPL's own returns: total revenue was $161K (year ending 2004-03), $130K (year ending 2005-03), $240K (year ending 2006-03), with no $450K receipt, so its timing and recipient are unconfirmed. The St. Martin's withdrawal (2006) involved no payment. DCPL's grant lists show awards from the ICG-funded and Monument funds. No cash settlement after about 2011 was found in HPRB actions, Mayor's Agent decisions or zoning exhibits searched — a null result, not proof of none. DCPL's 990s show program service revenue of $751K in the year ending 2014-03, far above other years; the 990 does not say what it was. Its year coincides with the Third Church demolition (2014), when ICG's payments would have come due — an inference from timing only.

## Comparison: New York, Philadelphia, Boston

All three run local programs comparable to DC's: a commission that designates individual landmarks and historic districts and reviews permits within them (New York's Landmarks Preservation Commission, 1965; the Philadelphia Historical Commission, 1955; the Boston Landmarks Commission, 1975, alongside separate district commissions). Each publishes designation polygons with dates.

DC has designated 16% of its land outside parks, 3.4 to 6.1 times the share in the other cities. Since 1990 the designated share outside parks grew by Boston +1.4 points, New York +1.2 points, Philadelphia +2.1 points, Washington +5.9 points. By buildings, DC has designated 21% outside parks against 3%–8% elsewhere. Buildings are each city's addressed buildings, one point each, counted as designated when the point falls in a designation: DC's active MAR address points flagged as buildings; New York's AddressPoint, one per building identification number; Boston's SAM addresses, one per building ID; Philadelphia's assessed properties other than vacant land, one per location, so a rowhouse counts once and condominium units collapse to their building. OpenStreetMap buildings (Geofabrik extracts of 2026-10-18) give similar shares (the OSM check column), though OSM's mapping differs by city: it maps Philadelphia's rowhouses one by one, appears to draw some of Boston's attached rows as one building (its Boston footprints are the largest of the four), and includes many of DC's outbuildings. Landmark counts are not like for like: New York counts landmark sites, Philadelphia individually registered parcels (many single rowhouses), Boston approved landmark petitions, DC Inventory landmarks. Areas use today's boundaries dated by each feature (New York's district extensions carry their own dates; the other cities' expansions count from the original date).
